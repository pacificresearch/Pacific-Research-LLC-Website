# First pass — PA-27-102 across all 24 participating institutes

**Question:** which three institutes are the best fit for Andrew's
background, ranked by budget cap and by how crowded the prior-award
landscape looks?

**Run date:** 2026-08-25 · `python3 sbir_sttr_pipeline.py --institute-scan PA-27-102`

**Announcement, verified:** PA-27-102, *NIH Small Business Technology
Transfer Grant (Parent STTR [R41/R42] Clinical Trial Optional)*, posted
May 28 2026, 24 participating components plus ORWH as a co-funding
office. Standard due dates **September 5 2026 · January 5 2027 ·
April 5 2027**, read from the announcement's own Key Dates table at
`https://grants.gov/grantsws/rest/opportunity/att/download/352559`.

**Budget caps, verified:** from that same announcement's IC budget
table. **SBA budgetary guideline: $323,090 Phase I / $2,153,927
Phase II**, read live from `https://www.sbir.gov/about` and
cross-checked against
`https://seed.nih.gov/small-business-funding/small-business-program-basics/understanding-sbir-sttr`.

**Prior awards:** NIH RePORTER v2, activity R41/R42, FY2021–FY2026, one
query per component, **deduplicated to distinct projects.** RePORTER
returns one row per project-year, so a Phase I, its Phase II and every
continuation share a core number; counting rows turns NIA's 114 projects
into 182 and inflates every concentration figure.

## The finding that reframes the question

**Across all 24 institutes, over six fiscal years, there are zero STTR
awards for clinical-trial-operations methods.**

Not few. Zero. Every award that the lane classifier flags anywhere in
the portfolio is a wearable, a remote-monitoring device, a digital-health
app, or a care-coordination platform. Nothing about site activation,
accrual, protocol deviation, source data verification, coordinator
burden, or trial conduct as a subject in its own right.

I checked whether that was an STTR artifact. It is not: across **2,064
distinct SBIR (R43/R44) projects** at nine institutes over the same six
years, exactly one title matches the trial-operations vocabulary — NCI's
*"Decentralized Clinical Trial of Effectiveness of EnergyPoints App…"* —
and even that is a **product being tested via a decentralized trial**,
not a method for running one.

So: **NIH does not fund "how trials run" through the small-business
programs.** Andrew's strongest and most differentiated asset is not, by
itself, a fundable topic here.

That is worth knowing before writing anything, and it has one clear
strategic consequence:

> The entry is a **funded topic** — digital health, remote monitoring,
> care coordination — **differentiated by operations credibility.** Not
> "here is a wearable," which 150 firms are already saying, but "here is
> the trial-conduct layer that makes a decentralized study actually run,
> from someone who has watched them fail at the site."

## The second finding: crowding is not the constraint

After deduplication, the field is far more open than the raw numbers
suggest. Top-three firm concentration at the large institutes:

| | NCI | NIA | NHLBI | NIMH |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| distinct projects | 180 | 114 | 130 | 44 |
| distinct firms | 150 | 102 | 110 | 38 |
| top-3 share | **5%** | **6%** | **8%** | **15%** |

NIH STTR is **not** captured by repeat winners. NCI's 180 projects are
spread across 150 firms. There is no "same three firms taking 40 awards"
pattern to steer around at any institute in the top cap tier.

The scarce resource is not access to the field. It is a **fundable
framing** — see the first finding.

## The trap: NCATS is the institute you would guess, and it is wrong

NCATS is the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences. It
runs the CTSA program, which is where Stanford's Spectrum unit sits. On
mission it looks like the perfect home for trial-operations methods.

Its actual STTR portfolio is 31 projects of bench and biologics tooling:
AAV neutralizing-antibody blockade, CHO-cell antibody production,
designer Pichia strains, intestinal organoid screening platforms,
microfluidic inactivation. **Not one** translational-methods award.

And it sits in the lowest cap tier — SBA guideline both phases
($323,090 / $2,153,927), less than half NCI's Phase I.

Best-looking mission fit, worst actual fit, worst budget. Do not start
here.

## The three, ranked

The two axes the question names pull against each other, so I am saying
so rather than hiding it. **By budget cap:** NIMH = NIA = NCI on Phase I
($700K), with NIMH and NIA beating NCI on Phase II ($3M vs $2.5M). **By
openness:** NCI (5%, 150 firms) > NIA (6%, 102) > NIMH (15%, 38). My
tiebreak is lane density and mission fit to what Andrew actually did,
which puts NIMH first.

### 1. NIMH — National Institute of Mental Health

**Cap: $700,000 Phase I / $3,000,000 Phase II** — top tier on both.
**Field: 44 distinct projects, 38 firms, top-3 at 15%.**
**Lane: 3 of 44 (6%)** — the highest lane density of any institute in
the top cap tier.

Why first: NIMH funds **behavioral-intervention delivery**, and its lane
awards say so — *"Facilitating Delivery and Adherence in Evidence-Based
Treatment…"*, *"A Digital Health Technology to Prevent Family Violence…"*.
Behavioral trials are precisely the trials where retention, adherence,
coordinator burden and protocol fidelity dominate the outcome. That is
the closest any institute gets to funding what Andrew knows, and it is
the shortest distance from a fundable topic to his differentiator.

Smallest portfolio in the top cap tier also means the least competition
for program-officer attention, which at this stage matters more than
competition for dollars.

**Named program officer: Mary Margaret Sweeney** (2 of the 3 lane
awards). Second: Margaret Christine Grabb.

**Caveat:** 15% top-3 concentration is the highest of the three, and 44
projects over six years is ~7/year. It is the tightest field of the
three by a real margin.

### 2. NIA — National Institute on Aging

**Cap: $700,000 Phase I / $3,000,000 Phase II** — top tier on both.
**Field: 114 distinct projects, 102 firms, top-3 at 6%** — very open.
**Lane: 3 of 114 (2%)** — remote patient monitoring, caregiver
monitoring, circadian profiling.

Why second: ADRD trials are the hardest recruitment-and-retention
problem in clinical research, and decentralized and remote conduct is
where NIA is already spending. A trial-conduct proposal aimed at
retention in an ADRD cohort is a topic NIA has a standing reason to
care about, at the top cap tier, in a field of 102 firms where nobody
holds more than a couple of awards.

More money moving than NIMH, nearly as open as NCI.

**Named program officers: Irina Y Sazonova, Melissa S Gerald,
Yuan Luo** (one lane award each — less concentrated PO signal than
NIMH's, so the pre-application call matters more here to find the right
one).

### 3. NCI — National Cancer Institute

**Cap: $700,000 Phase I / $2,500,000 Phase II** — top tier Phase I,
**one tier down on Phase II.**
**Field: 180 distinct projects, 150 firms, top-3 at 5%** — the most
money and the most open field in absolute terms.
**Lane: 3 of 180 (1%)** — including *"A cloud-based digital health
navigation program for colorectal cancer screening."*

Why third despite the biggest portfolio: **patient navigation** is
genuinely trial-adjacent operations work and NCI funds it, which is a
real opening. But NCI's portfolio is overwhelmingly therapeutics and
imaging devices, so an operations-flavoured proposal competes hardest
here for *attention* even while competing least for *money*. And the
$2.5M Phase II ceiling is $500K below NIMH and NIA.

**Named program officer: Ming Zhao** (2 of the 3 lane awards).

## Why not the others

- **NHLBI** — $3M Phase II is top tier, but **Phase I is $400,000**, one
  tier down. PRG enters at Phase I, so the Phase I ceiling is the one
  that binds first. Otherwise a strong institute: 130 projects, 110
  firms, top-3 at 8%.
- **NINR** — the **highest lane density anywhere (2 of 10, 20%)** and the
  closest mission on care delivery. But 10 distinct projects over six
  years is under two a year, at $400K/$2.5M. A real option if the topic
  lands squarely on care coordination; too thin a base rate to build a
  first application around.
- **NIAID** — 182 projects at $700K/$3M, but **zero** in lane. It funds
  infectious-disease and immunology product development.
- **NIGMS, NINDS** — top cap tier, 94 and 74 projects, but 1 lane award
  each. Nothing to anchor to.
- **NIMHD** — 11% lane density on health-disparities work, which touches
  Andrew's Portuguese-language and refugee/evacuee operations
  experience. Lowest cap tier (SBA guideline both phases) and 17
  projects. Worth watching, not worth leading with.
- **ORIP and ORWH** — zero STTR awards in six years.
- **NLM** — 3 projects, top-3 at 100%. Effectively closed.

## Two things to flag

**1. PA-27-102 now prohibits foreign subawards outright.** Not a
disclosure requirement — a bar:

> *"NIH will no longer issue awards (i.e., new, renewal, or non-competing
> continuation) to domestic or foreign entities that involve foreign
> subawards/subcontracts… Applications involving foreign
> subawards/subcontracts submitted in response to this NOFO will be
> deemed noncompliant and will not be considered for funding."*

Unfunded international collaborations, foreign consultants, and foreign
vendor purchases are still allowed. PRG is clean on all of it, but this
closes the door on any structure that would have routed money to a
partner in Brazil or Peru.

**2. The PD/PI rule, in the announcement's own words** — this is what
makes the whole lane work while the job search is live:

> *"For the STTR program, the PD(s)/PI(s) may be employed with the SBC or
> the single, 'partnering' non-profit research institution as long as
> s/he has a formal appointment with or commitment to the applicant SBC…
> Each PD/PI must commit a minimum of 10% effort… Such a relationship
> does not necessarily involve a salary or other form of remuneration."*

And the split that has to be budgeted before anything is promised: **at
least 40%** of the research effort by PRG, **at least 30%** by the single
partnering institution.

## What I would do next

1. **Call Mary Margaret Sweeney at NIMH.** One call, before writing
   anything. The question is not "would you fund this" — it is "your
   portfolio funds intervention delivery and adherence; is the conduct
   layer underneath that in scope, and is there a NOSI coming?" That
   call is free and most applicants never make it.
2. **Ask Stanford Department of Medicine for the PD/PI** — Quantitative
   Sciences Unit or the Spectrum CTSA unit. Nothing names anyone in any
   document until they agree in writing.
3. **Target January 5 2027, not September 5 2026.** Sep 5 is 11 days
   out; a first STTR with an IP allocation agreement to negotiate needs
   90+. Jan 5 is 133 days out, which is enough.
4. **Watch PAR-27-040** — *Small Business Transition Grant for New
   Entrepreneurs (R41/R42)*, the one survivor of the first pipeline run.
   It restricts the PD/PI to people who have **never** been an
   independent PD/PI on an NIH grant. That is Andrew exactly, and it
   removes every established small-business PI from the field. It is
   forecast only — NIH has stated intent, the NOFO is not published —
   with an estimated January 5 2027 date. Nothing to write against yet;
   everything to preposition for.
